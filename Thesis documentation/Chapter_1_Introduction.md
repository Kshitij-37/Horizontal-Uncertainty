# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 The Role of Wind Energy in the Energy Transition

The global energy system is undergoing a fundamental transformation. Driven by the imperative to limit anthropogenic climate change, nations around the world have committed to reducing greenhouse gas emissions from fossil fuel combustion and replacing carbon-intensive power generation with renewable energy sources. The Paris Agreement, adopted by 196 parties at the 21st Conference of the Parties (COP21) in December 2015, established the goal of limiting global average temperature rise to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius [12]. Achieving these targets requires a rapid and sustained expansion of renewable energy capacity across all regions of the world.

Wind energy has emerged as one of the central pillars of this energy transition. Among all renewable energy technologies, wind power offers a compelling combination of technological maturity, cost competitiveness, and scalability. Over the past two decades, advances in turbine design, manufacturing processes, and project development practices have driven the levelised cost of energy (LCOE) for onshore wind to levels that are competitive with, and in many markets below, the cost of new fossil fuel generation. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), the global weighted-average LCOE for onshore wind fell by approximately 69% between 2010 and 2022, making it one of the cheapest sources of new electricity generation available today [7].

The growth of installed wind energy capacity over the past fifteen years has been remarkable. According to the Global Wind Energy Council (GWEC), cumulative global installed wind power capacity grew from approximately 198 GW at the end of 2010 to over 1,021 GW by the end of 2023, representing a more than fivefold increase in just over a decade [6]. Of this total, onshore wind accounts for the vast majority -- approximately 880 GW, or roughly 86% of all installed wind capacity. Annual onshore wind installations have grown from around 36 GW per year in 2010 to over 100 GW per year in recent years, with China, the United States, Brazil, Germany, and India leading the way in new capacity additions [6]. The GWEC's projections indicate that annual installations will need to increase further -- to approximately 150 GW per year by 2030 -- if the global energy sector is to align with a pathway consistent with the Paris Agreement's temperature targets.

It is important to distinguish between onshore and offshore wind energy, as the two sectors differ significantly in terms of technology, cost structure, development timelines, and the technical challenges they present. Onshore wind farms are installed on land, typically in areas with favourable wind resources such as ridgelines, open plains, coastal zones, or elevated terrain. Offshore wind farms are installed in bodies of water, either on fixed foundations in shallow waters or on floating platforms in deeper waters. While offshore wind has seen rapid growth in recent years -- particularly in the North Sea, the Baltic Sea, and the coastal waters of East Asia -- onshore wind remains the dominant technology globally and is the focus of this thesis. The methods and models developed in this work are specifically designed for onshore wind resource assessment, where terrain effects play a critical role in determining wind conditions at individual turbine locations.

The development of a wind farm is a capital-intensive undertaking. A typical onshore wind project requires a capital expenditure (CAPEX) on the order of 1 to 2 million euros per megawatt of installed capacity, depending on the turbine technology, site conditions, and local infrastructure requirements [7]. For a medium-sized wind farm of 50 MW, this implies a total investment of 50 to 100 million euros. Wind farm projects are typically financed on a project finance basis, meaning that the debt and equity used to fund the project are repaid from the project's own cash flows -- specifically, the revenue generated by selling the electricity produced by the wind turbines over the project's operational lifetime, which is typically 20 to 25 years. This long payback period, combined with the large upfront investment, means that the financial viability of a wind farm project depends critically on the accuracy with which the project's future energy production can be predicted.

This brings us to the concept of bankability. For a wind farm project to secure financing from banks and institutional investors, the project developer must demonstrate that the project will generate sufficient revenue to service its debt obligations and provide an acceptable return on equity, even under adverse conditions. The primary risk in wind energy project finance is production risk -- the possibility that the wind farm will produce less electricity than predicted. To assess this risk, lenders and investors rely on independent energy yield assessments (EYAs), which provide estimates of the project's expected annual energy production along with a quantification of the uncertainty associated with that estimate.

The uncertainty in the energy yield estimate is expressed through a set of probability values, commonly referred to as P-values. The P50 value represents the best estimate of annual energy production -- the level that is expected to be exceeded in 50% of years. The P75 value is the production level expected to be exceeded in 75% of years, and the P90 value is expected to be exceeded in 90% of years. Banks typically use the P90 value to size the project's debt: the annual debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) must meet a minimum threshold (usually 1.2 to 1.4) even at the P90 production level. Equity investors, meanwhile, evaluate their returns at the P75 level. The relationship between these P-values and the total uncertainty in the energy yield estimate is direct and consequential: higher uncertainty leads to lower P75 and P90 values, which in turn reduce the amount of debt the project can support and the returns available to equity investors. In extreme cases, excessive uncertainty can render a project unbankable -- unable to secure financing on acceptable terms.

It is within this context that the present thesis is situated. Among the many components of total energy yield uncertainty, one of the most significant -- and one of the least well understood in a quantitative, site-specific sense -- is the uncertainty associated with horizontal extrapolation: the process of transferring the observed wind climate from a measurement location to the individual turbine positions within a wind farm using a wind flow model. This uncertainty is particularly challenging because it depends on a complex interplay of terrain characteristics, atmospheric conditions, measurement configurations, and flow model limitations. The central aim of this thesis is to develop a data-driven Bayesian model that can predict the magnitude of this horizontal extrapolation uncertainty as a function of measurable terrain and site characteristics, thereby providing project developers and financial institutions with more accurate, site-specific uncertainty estimates than are currently available from conventional industry practice.


## 1.2 Energy Yield Assessment: The Industry Pipeline

### 1.2.1 Overview of the Energy Yield Assessment Process

An energy yield assessment (EYA) is the cornerstone of wind farm project development and financing. It is a comprehensive technical study that estimates the long-term average annual energy production (AEP) of a proposed wind farm, along with the uncertainty associated with that estimate. The EYA is typically conducted by an independent technical advisor -- a consultancy with recognised expertise in wind energy -- and the results are relied upon by project developers, lenders, equity investors, and insurers in making their investment decisions. The quality, rigour, and credibility of the EYA directly influence the financial terms available to the project.

The EYA process follows a well-established pipeline of sequential steps, each of which introduces its own sources of uncertainty. Understanding this pipeline is essential for appreciating where horizontal extrapolation uncertainty fits within the broader picture and why its accurate quantification matters. The following subsections describe each step of the pipeline in detail.

### 1.2.2 Site Prospecting and Preliminary Assessment

The EYA process begins with site prospecting: the identification of locations with potentially favourable wind resources for wind farm development. Site prospecting involves a combination of mesoscale wind resource mapping (using reanalysis data such as ERA5 or MERRA-2, or commercial mesoscale models such as the Global Wind Atlas), analysis of existing wind data from nearby meteorological stations, assessment of terrain characteristics (elevation, roughness, exposure), evaluation of grid connection possibilities, and consideration of environmental and planning constraints. The output of the prospecting phase is a shortlist of candidate sites that warrant further investigation through dedicated on-site wind measurements.

At this stage, a preliminary energy yield estimate may be produced using mesoscale wind data and assumed turbine parameters. This estimate is subject to large uncertainties (often 15-25%) and is used only for initial screening and business case evaluation, not for financing purposes.

### 1.2.3 Measurement Campaign Design and Execution

Once a candidate site has been selected for development, the next step is the design and installation of an on-site measurement campaign. The purpose of the measurement campaign is to collect high-quality wind data at the site over a sufficiently long period to characterise the site's wind resource and enable its correlation with long-term reference data.

The measurement campaign typically consists of one or more meteorological masts (met masts) instrumented with a suite of sensors. A typical met mast configuration includes:

- **Cup anemometers** at multiple heights, including at or near the proposed turbine hub height. Cup anemometers are the industry standard for measuring mean wind speed, as specified by the IEC 61400-12-1 standard [9]. Top-mounted anemometers are preferred because they are less affected by flow distortion from the mast structure.
- **Wind vanes** at one or more heights to measure wind direction. Wind direction measurements are essential for characterising the directional distribution of the wind resource and for computing sector-wise statistics.
- **Temperature and pressure sensors** to enable air density corrections. Air density directly affects the power output of a wind turbine for a given wind speed, so accurate density measurements are important for energy production estimates.
- **Additional sensors** such as humidity sensors, rain gauges, and pyranometers (solar radiation) may be included depending on the project's requirements and the prevailing climate.

In recent years, remote sensing devices -- particularly ground-based LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) systems -- have increasingly been used as a complement to or replacement for met masts. LiDAR systems can measure wind speed and direction at multiple heights simultaneously, providing detailed vertical profiles of the wind resource. They offer advantages in terms of measurement flexibility (no tall structure required) and the ability to measure at hub heights that exceed the practical limit for met masts (typically around 100-120 metres). However, LiDAR measurements have their own uncertainty characteristics, including sensitivity to atmospheric conditions (precipitation, turbulence) and potential biases in complex terrain due to flow inhomogeneity across the measurement volume.

The measurement campaign duration is typically 12 to 24 months. A minimum of 12 months is generally required to capture the full seasonal cycle of wind variability, while longer campaigns (18-24 months) provide more robust statistics and reduce the uncertainty associated with the long-term correction step. The campaign must achieve a high data recovery rate -- typically 90% or more -- to be considered representative of the site's wind climate.

### 1.2.4 Data Quality Control and Filtering

Raw data from the measurement campaign undergo rigorous quality control (QC) procedures before being used in the EYA. The QC process includes:

- **Sensor health monitoring:** Identifying periods of sensor malfunction, icing, calibration drift, or other anomalies that compromise data quality.
- **Data filtering:** Removing or flagging data records affected by identifiable problems, such as tower shadow effects (wind speed reductions when the anemometer is in the wake of the mast), sensor icing (indicated by unusual relationships between temperature and wind speed), and electrical interference.
- **Consistency checks:** Comparing data from redundant sensors (e.g., paired anemometers on opposite sides of the mast) and between different measurement heights to identify inconsistencies.
- **Statistical screening:** Identifying and investigating outliers, sudden jumps, or unrealistic values in the time series.

The quality-controlled dataset forms the basis for all subsequent analysis. The integrity of this dataset is fundamental to the accuracy of the EYA; systematic biases introduced by measurement errors propagate through all downstream calculations.

### 1.2.5 Long-Term Correction (Measure-Correlate-Predict)

On-site measurement campaigns capture only a snapshot of the site's wind resource -- typically one to two years. However, the EYA must estimate the long-term average energy production over the project's 20-25 year lifetime. Since wind resources exhibit significant inter-annual variability (year-to-year variations in annual mean wind speed can be 5-10% or more), it is essential to adjust the on-site data to reflect long-term average conditions.

This adjustment is performed through a process known as long-term correction or Measure-Correlate-Predict (MCP). The MCP process involves three steps:

1. **Correlate:** Establish a statistical relationship between the on-site measurements and a concurrent long-term reference dataset. The reference dataset may come from nearby meteorological stations with long records (10-30 years), global reanalysis products (e.g., ERA5, MERRA-2), or virtual mast data from mesoscale models.
2. **Predict:** Use the established relationship, together with the full historical record of the reference dataset, to predict what the on-site wind resource would have been over the entire reference period.
3. **Adjust:** Derive the long-term average wind resource at the site from the predicted long-term time series or statistical distributions.

Several MCP methodologies are used in practice, including linear regression, variance ratio methods, matrix methods, and more advanced approaches such as the Weibull-based method. Each method has different assumptions and limitations, and the choice of method and reference data introduces its own uncertainty into the EYA.

### 1.2.6 Wind Resource Assessment: Horizontal Extrapolation

With the long-term corrected wind resource characterised at the measurement location(s), the next step is to transfer this wind climate to the individual turbine positions within the wind farm. This process is called horizontal extrapolation, and it is the central topic of this thesis. The process uses a wind flow model -- most commonly WAsP (Wind Atlas Analysis and Application Program) -- to account for the effects of terrain (orography and surface roughness) on wind flow between the measurement and turbine locations.

The horizontal extrapolation step is often the largest single source of uncertainty in the EYA, particularly in complex terrain where terrain effects are strong and difficult to model accurately. Section 1.3 discusses horizontal extrapolation and wind flow modelling in detail.

### 1.2.7 Energy Production Estimation

Once the predicted wind speed distribution is known at each turbine location (typically expressed as a Weibull distribution characterised by its A (scale) and k (shape) parameters for each wind direction sector), the expected energy production is calculated by combining the wind speed distribution with the turbine's power curve.

The power curve describes the relationship between wind speed and electrical power output for a given turbine model. It is provided by the turbine manufacturer and is typically defined for standard air density conditions (1.225 kg/m^3 at sea level). The predicted energy production for each turbine is computed by integrating the product of the wind speed probability density function and the power curve over all wind speeds and direction sectors:

AEP = 8766 * sum over all sectors of [frequency_sector * integral from cut-in to cut-out of P(v) * f(v) dv]

where AEP is the annual energy production in kWh, 8766 is the number of hours in an average year, P(v) is the power curve (electrical output as a function of wind speed v), f(v) is the Weibull probability density function of wind speed in each sector, and frequency_sector is the fraction of time the wind blows from each direction sector.

Air density corrections are applied to adjust the power curve for the actual air density at the site, which depends on the site's elevation above sea level and ambient temperature.

### 1.2.8 Loss Estimation

The gross energy production calculated in the previous step represents the theoretical maximum output under idealised conditions. In practice, a wind farm's net energy production is reduced by a series of losses. The EYA must estimate each of these losses to arrive at the net AEP:

- **Wake losses:** When turbines are arranged in a wind farm, downstream turbines operate in the wake of upstream turbines. The wake is characterised by reduced wind speed and increased turbulence, both of which reduce power output. Wake losses depend on the wind farm layout, turbine spacing, turbine rotor diameter, and the prevailing wind direction distribution. Typical wake losses range from 5% to 15% of gross production, depending on the layout and wind rose.
- **Electrical losses:** Losses in the internal cabling, transformers, substation, and grid connection. These are typically in the range of 2-3% of gross production.
- **Availability losses:** Losses due to turbine downtime for scheduled maintenance, unscheduled repairs, and grid outages. Availability is typically guaranteed by the turbine manufacturer at 95-98%, implying availability losses of 2-5%.
- **Curtailment losses:** Losses due to deliberate reduction of turbine output for reasons such as noise compliance, shadow flicker limits, grid congestion, or environmental restrictions (e.g., bat protection shutdowns). Curtailment losses vary widely depending on local regulations and grid conditions.
- **Icing losses:** In cold climates, ice accretion on turbine blades reduces aerodynamic efficiency and may require turbine shutdown for safety reasons. Icing losses can be significant in Nordic countries and at high altitudes.
- **Degradation and other losses:** Minor losses due to blade soiling, yaw misalignment, control system inefficiencies, and long-term performance degradation.

### 1.2.9 Uncertainty Analysis

The uncertainty analysis is a critical component of the EYA. It quantifies the confidence in the energy yield estimate by combining all individual sources of uncertainty into a total uncertainty value, expressed as a standard deviation (sigma) of the predicted AEP.

The major categories of uncertainty in an EYA are as follows:

**Measurement uncertainty** encompasses all sources of error in the on-site wind measurements. This includes anemometer calibration uncertainty (typically 0.5-1% of wind speed for well-calibrated cup anemometers), mounting effects (flow distortion from the mast structure, typically 0.5-1%), data recovery gaps, and sensor-specific biases. For LiDAR measurements, additional uncertainties arise from the measurement principle itself, including volume-averaging effects and sensitivity to atmospheric conditions. The combined measurement uncertainty is typically in the range of 1-3% of annual energy production.

**Long-term correction uncertainty** reflects the limitations of the MCP process. It includes uncertainty in the reference data (quality, representativeness, and length of the reference record), uncertainty in the correlation methodology (model selection, extrapolation beyond the range of calibration data), and the fundamental uncertainty associated with inter-annual wind resource variability that cannot be captured by a finite reference record. This component is typically 2-4% of AEP, with larger values for shorter measurement campaigns and weaker correlations with reference data.

**Horizontal extrapolation uncertainty** is the uncertainty associated with transferring the wind resource from the measurement location to the turbine positions using a wind flow model. This is the uncertainty component that is the primary focus of this thesis. It depends on the complexity of the terrain, the distance between the measurement and prediction sites, the quality of the terrain input data, and the limitations of the flow model itself. In flat terrain with simple roughness conditions, horizontal extrapolation uncertainty may be as low as 2% of AEP, while in complex terrain with steep slopes, heterogeneous roughness, and large distances between the measurement mast and turbine positions, it can exceed 8% of AEP.

**Vertical extrapolation uncertainty** arises when the measurement height differs from the turbine hub height, requiring an extrapolation of the wind profile from the measurement height to the hub height. This extrapolation is typically performed using a logarithmic or power law wind profile, or using the WAsP model's profile calculations. The uncertainty depends on the height difference, the complexity of the vertical wind profile (which is influenced by atmospheric stability, surface roughness, and terrain effects), and the quality of the wind profile measurements available from the met mast. Typical values range from 1-3% of AEP.

**Power curve uncertainty** reflects the difference between the manufacturer's warranted power curve and the actual performance of the turbine in the field. This difference can arise from variations in blade aerodynamics, turbulence effects, air density corrections, and control system settings. Power curve uncertainty is typically 2-5% of AEP.

**Loss estimation uncertainty** encompasses the uncertainties in the wake loss model, availability assumptions, curtailment estimates, and other loss components. Wake model uncertainty is typically 1-3% of AEP, while uncertainties in other loss categories are generally smaller.

The total uncertainty is calculated by combining the individual uncertainty components. When the individual uncertainties are independent (uncorrelated), the total uncertainty is the root-sum-square of the components:

sigma_total = sqrt(sigma_measurement^2 + sigma_MCP^2 + sigma_horizontal^2 + sigma_vertical^2 + sigma_powercurve^2 + sigma_losses^2)

where each sigma_i is expressed as a percentage of annual energy production. In practice, some uncertainty components may be partially correlated (e.g., measurement uncertainty and vertical extrapolation uncertainty both involve the measured wind profile), and adjustments may be made to account for these correlations.

### 1.2.10 P-Values and Their Financial Significance

The total uncertainty from the EYA is used to derive probability-weighted energy production estimates, known as P-values. These P-values are the fundamental inputs to the financial model that determines the project's bankability.

The P-value framework assumes that the annual energy production follows a normal distribution (or approximately normal, given the central limit theorem applied to the many sources of variation). The P-value at exceedance probability x is calculated as:

P_x = P50 * (1 - z_x * sigma_total)

where P50 is the best estimate of annual energy production (the mean or median of the distribution), z_x is the standard normal quantile corresponding to exceedance probability x, and sigma_total is the total uncertainty expressed as a fraction (not a percentage). The key quantiles used in practice are:

- P50: z = 0 (50% exceedance probability). This is the best estimate, expected to be exceeded in half of all years.
- P75: z = 0.674 (75% exceedance probability). This value is commonly used by equity investors to evaluate their expected returns.
- P90: z = 1.282 (90% exceedance probability). This value is used by lenders to size the project's debt and calculate the debt service coverage ratio.

To illustrate the financial significance of uncertainty, consider a project with a P50 energy yield of 100 GWh/year and a total uncertainty of 8% (sigma_total = 0.08):

- P75 = 100 * (1 - 0.674 * 0.08) = 100 * 0.946 = 94.6 GWh/year
- P90 = 100 * (1 - 1.282 * 0.08) = 100 * 0.897 = 89.7 GWh/year

Now consider the same project with a total uncertainty of 10% (sigma_total = 0.10):

- P75 = 100 * (1 - 0.674 * 0.10) = 100 * 0.933 = 93.3 GWh/year
- P90 = 100 * (1 - 1.282 * 0.10) = 100 * 0.872 = 87.2 GWh/year

The increase in total uncertainty from 8% to 10% -- just two percentage points -- reduces the P90 from 89.7 GWh to 87.2 GWh, a decrease of 2.5 GWh/year. At a typical electricity price of 50 EUR/MWh, this represents a reduction in annual P90 revenue of 125,000 EUR per year, or 2.5 million EUR over the 20-year project lifetime. For a highly leveraged project, this reduction can materially affect the debt service coverage ratio and may require either a reduction in the debt amount (requiring more equity, which is more expensive) or an increase in the electricity price (which may not be achievable in a competitive market).

More critically, a 1 percentage point increase in sigma_total reduces P90 by approximately 1.3 percentage points of P50 (since the P90 sensitivity is z_90 * delta_sigma = 1.282 * 0.01 = 0.013). For a borderline project -- one where the DSCR at P90 is close to the minimum threshold -- this difference can be the margin between a bankable and an unbankable project. This is why accurate quantification of each uncertainty component matters, and why the horizontal extrapolation uncertainty -- which can range from 2% to 8% depending on terrain complexity and site configuration -- represents both a significant risk and a significant opportunity for improved estimation.

## 1.3 Horizontal Extrapolation and Wind Flow Modelling

### 1.3.1 Definition and Purpose of Horizontal Extrapolation

Horizontal extrapolation is the process of transferring the observed wind climate from a measurement site -- typically a meteorological mast (referred to throughout this thesis as the "predictor" or "MM" site) -- to a target site (referred to as the "predicted" or "WTG" site) that may be located several hundred metres to several kilometres away. The target site is usually the position of a wind turbine within the planned wind farm layout.

The need for horizontal extrapolation arises from a fundamental practical constraint: it is not economically feasible to install a measurement mast at every turbine position within a wind farm. A typical onshore wind farm may contain 10 to 50 turbines, distributed over an area of several square kilometres, while the measurement campaign typically involves only one to three meteorological masts. The wind conditions at the turbine positions must therefore be inferred from the measurements at the mast locations, taking into account the effects of terrain on wind flow between the two locations.

This inference is performed using a wind flow model -- a mathematical model that simulates the effects of terrain on the atmospheric boundary layer wind flow. The model takes as input the observed wind climate at the measurement site and the terrain characteristics (topography and surface roughness) of both the measurement and target sites, and produces as output the predicted wind climate at the target site. The accuracy of this prediction depends on the quality of the input data, the physical fidelity of the flow model, and the degree to which the terrain characteristics satisfy the model's underlying assumptions.

### 1.3.2 Physical Basis of Terrain Effects on Wind Flow

To understand the challenges of horizontal extrapolation and the limitations of the flow models used to perform it, it is necessary to review the physical mechanisms by which terrain influences the wind flow in the atmospheric boundary layer.

**The Atmospheric Boundary Layer.** The atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) is the lowest portion of the atmosphere, extending from the Earth's surface to a height of approximately 500 to 2000 metres, depending on atmospheric stability and surface conditions. Within the ABL, the wind flow is directly influenced by the Earth's surface through friction, heat exchange, and moisture transfer. The wind speed increases with height above the surface due to the decreasing influence of surface friction. This increase is commonly described by either the logarithmic wind profile (derived from Monin-Obukhov similarity theory) or the power law profile:

Logarithmic profile: u(z) = (u* / kappa) * ln(z / z0)

where u(z) is the wind speed at height z, u* is the friction velocity, kappa is the von Karman constant (approximately 0.4), and z0 is the aerodynamic roughness length.

Power law profile: u(z) = u(z_ref) * (z / z_ref)^alpha

where z_ref is a reference height and alpha is the wind shear exponent, which depends on surface roughness and atmospheric stability.

**Surface Roughness.** The aerodynamic roughness length z0 characterises the effect of the Earth's surface on the wind profile. Different land cover types have very different roughness lengths: open water has z0 on the order of 0.0001 to 0.001 metres, short grass has z0 of approximately 0.01 to 0.03 metres, agricultural land with crops has z0 of 0.05 to 0.3 metres, forested areas have z0 of 0.5 to 1.5 metres, and urban areas may have z0 exceeding 1 metre [1]. When the wind flows from one roughness type to another (a roughness change), an internal boundary layer (IBL) develops, within which the wind profile adjusts from the upstream roughness characteristics to the downstream roughness characteristics. This adjustment is gradual and extends over distances of several kilometres, depending on the height of interest and the magnitude of the roughness change. Accounting for these roughness transitions is one of the key tasks of a wind flow model.

**Orographic Effects.** The topography of the terrain -- hills, ridges, valleys, cliffs, and escarpments -- has a profound effect on the local wind flow. When air flows over a hill or ridge, it accelerates on the windward slope (the speedup effect), reaches its maximum speed near the crest, and decelerates on the leeward slope. The physics of this orographic speedup was first described systematically by Jackson and Hunt [10], who developed an analytical solution for the linearised flow over a low, two-dimensional hill. Their key result is that the fractional speedup at the summit of a hill is approximately proportional to the ratio of the hill height to its half-length (H/L), where the half-length is the horizontal distance from the summit to the point where the elevation has decreased to half the hill height. The Jackson-Hunt theory forms the physical foundation of the WAsP model's orographic correction.

In addition to speedup, terrain features can cause deflection of the wind direction. Channelling effects in valleys, the Coriolis effect acting on flow disturbances, and pressure gradients induced by complex terrain geometries can all cause the wind to turn as it flows through complex topography. The magnitude and direction of this turning depend on the three-dimensional geometry of the terrain and the scale of the terrain features relative to the atmospheric boundary layer depth.

**Flow Separation and Recirculation.** When the terrain slope exceeds a critical threshold -- approximately 0.3, or about 17 degrees from the horizontal -- the flow can no longer follow the terrain surface smoothly. Instead, the boundary layer separates from the surface on the lee side of the hill, creating a region of recirculating flow (a separation bubble) with highly turbulent, low-speed conditions. Flow separation is a fundamentally non-linear phenomenon that cannot be captured by linearised flow models such as WAsP. The occurrence of flow separation is one of the primary reasons for increased prediction errors in complex terrain.

**Atmospheric Stability.** The vertical temperature profile of the atmosphere influences the degree of turbulent mixing within the boundary layer. In neutral conditions (the temperature decreases with height at the dry adiabatic lapse rate), turbulent mixing is determined primarily by wind shear. In stable conditions (temperature inversion, typically occurring at night in clear skies), turbulent mixing is suppressed, leading to a shallower boundary layer, reduced vertical momentum transport, and a wind profile that deviates from the neutral logarithmic profile. In unstable conditions (surface heating, typically occurring during daytime with solar insolation), turbulent mixing is enhanced, leading to a deeper boundary layer and a more uniform wind profile. Atmospheric stability affects the wind profile, the response of the flow to terrain perturbations, and the development of internal boundary layers at roughness changes. Most linearised flow models, including WAsP, were originally developed for neutral stability conditions, with empirical stability corrections added later.

### 1.3.3 The WAsP Model

The Wind Atlas Analysis and Application Program (WAsP) is the industry standard software tool for wind resource assessment and horizontal extrapolation in the wind energy industry. Developed at Riso National Laboratory (now DTU Wind Energy) in Denmark, WAsP was first released in 1987 and has since been used in thousands of wind energy projects worldwide [1]. Its widespread adoption and long track record make it the de facto standard for wind flow modelling in onshore wind energy, and its predictions form the basis of the vast majority of bankable EYAs produced globally.

WAsP is based on a linearised model of the atmospheric boundary layer flow over terrain. The fundamental approach follows a two-step procedure that is central to the Wind Atlas methodology:

**Step 1: Generalisation (Analysis).** The observed wind climate at the measurement site is "generalised" by removing the estimated effects of the local terrain. This involves computing the terrain corrections at the measurement site -- orographic speedup, roughness effects, obstacle sheltering, and stability effects -- and mathematically reversing them to obtain the "regional wind climate" that would exist in the absence of the specific terrain features near the measurement site. The regional wind climate is expressed as a set of Weibull distribution parameters (A and k) for each direction sector at each of several standard heights, representing the wind climate that would exist over a hypothetical flat, homogeneous surface.

**Step 2: Application (Synthesis).** The generalised regional wind climate is then "applied" at the target site by computing the terrain corrections at the target location and applying them to the regional wind climate. This produces the predicted wind climate at the target site, expressed as Weibull parameters for each sector.

The four terrain corrections applied in WAsP are:

1. **Orographic speedup model:** WAsP uses the BZ (Beljaars-Zilitinkevich) model, which is an extension of the Jackson-Hunt linearised theory for flow over low hills. The model computes the fractional speedup at each point in the domain based on the surrounding terrain topography. The model solves linearised equations of motion in the inner and outer layers of the boundary layer perturbation, using the terrain elevation data as input. The speedup is computed separately for each wind direction sector, reflecting the directional dependence of orographic effects.

2. **Roughness change model:** WAsP models the effect of roughness changes using the Internal Boundary Layer (IBL) theory. When the wind flows across a transition from one roughness type to another, an IBL develops, within which the wind profile adjusts to the new surface conditions. WAsP tracks these IBL transitions in all directions from both the measurement and target sites and computes the resulting modification of the wind profile at the height of interest.

3. **Obstacle sheltering model:** Nearby obstacles such as buildings, tree lines, and cliffs that are too small to be represented in the roughness map but large enough to affect the local wind flow are modelled as discrete sheltering elements. The user defines the position, dimensions, and porosity of each obstacle, and WAsP computes the resulting wind speed reduction at the measurement or target site.

4. **Stability correction:** WAsP includes empirical corrections for the effect of atmospheric stability on the wind profile and the terrain corrections. The stability correction is parameterised by the heat flux at the surface, which can be either specified by the user or estimated from a stability model.

WAsP outputs per direction sector (typically 12 sectors of 30 degrees each) include the speedup factor (the ratio of the predicted wind speed at the target site to the regional wind speed) and the deflection angle (the turning of the wind direction due to terrain effects). These sector-wise parameters are applied to the regional wind climate to produce the predicted wind speed distribution at the target site.

### 1.3.4 The WAsP Similarity Principle

A concept that is fundamental to understanding WAsP's behaviour and its limitations is the WAsP Similarity Principle. As stated by Landberg et al. [2], the key insight is that "the horizontal distance between the predictor and the predicted sites does not appear on the list [of similarity criteria]; one should much rather use similarity in regional wind climate." In other words, WAsP's accuracy does not depend on the physical distance between the measurement and prediction sites per se, but rather on whether the two sites share the same regional wind climate -- the same large-scale wind patterns that, once corrected for local terrain effects, should produce identical generalised wind climates.

The Similarity Principle implies that two sites that are far apart geographically but share the same regional wind climate (e.g., two sites in the same broad valley system or on the same plateau) should, in theory, yield equally accurate predictions as two sites that are close together. Conversely, two sites that are close together but separated by a significant change in regional wind climate (e.g., by a major mountain range or a coastline) may yield poor predictions despite their proximity.

In practice, however, distance between the sites does matter, for several reasons:

- **Mesoscale climate gradients:** Even within a relatively homogeneous region, the large-scale wind climate can vary over distances of tens of kilometres due to mesoscale effects such as sea-land breezes, valley winds, thermal circulations, and orographic blocking. These gradients violate the Similarity Principle's assumption of a uniform regional wind climate.
- **Terrain data resolution:** The accuracy of WAsP's terrain corrections depends on the resolution and quality of the input terrain data. Over longer distances, the probability of encountering terrain features that are not adequately resolved by the available data increases.
- **Unresolved terrain effects:** WAsP's linearised model cannot capture all terrain effects, particularly non-linear phenomena such as flow separation, thermal effects, and interactions between complex terrain features. The probability of encountering such effects increases with the complexity and extent of the terrain between the sites.
- **Roughness data quality:** Roughness maps are inherently simplified representations of the land surface, and their accuracy tends to decrease over longer distances as the roughness classification becomes less representative.

Therefore, while the Similarity Principle provides an important conceptual framework, the practical accuracy of WAsP predictions tends to degrade with increasing distance between the measurement and prediction sites, particularly in complex terrain. Quantifying this degradation -- and the uncertainty associated with it -- is one of the central motivations for this thesis.

### 1.3.5 WAsP Operating Envelope and Limitations

The WAsP model was designed for, and performs best in, terrain conditions that satisfy its linearised flow assumptions. The key limitations of the model are:

- **Slope limitation:** The linearised flow model is valid for terrain slopes up to approximately 0.3 (about 17 degrees). For steeper slopes, the flow can separate from the surface, creating regions of recirculation and high turbulence that the linear model cannot represent. In practice, WAsP may produce unreliable predictions at sites where the local terrain includes slopes exceeding this threshold.
- **Neutral stability assumption:** The core flow model assumes neutral atmospheric stability. While stability corrections are available in WAsP, they are empirical and may not fully capture the complex effects of stability on flow over terrain, particularly in cases of strong stable or unstable stratification.
- **Terrain data requirements:** The accuracy of WAsP's predictions depends critically on the quality and resolution of the input terrain data (elevation maps and roughness maps). Coarse or inaccurate terrain data can lead to significant errors in the computed terrain corrections. The recommended terrain data resolution depends on the terrain complexity but is typically 10-50 metres for the elevation data and should cover a radius of at least 10-20 kilometres around the sites of interest.
- **Homogeneous roughness assumption within sectors:** WAsP divides the terrain into roughness sectors and assumes relatively uniform roughness within each sector. In reality, roughness can vary continuously and heterogeneously across the landscape, and the simplified sectoral representation may not capture all relevant roughness transitions.
- **No mesoscale effects:** WAsP does not model mesoscale atmospheric phenomena such as thermal circulations, sea breezes, gap flows, or mountain waves. These phenomena can significantly affect the wind climate at scales of 10-100 kilometres and may cause the regional wind climate to vary between the measurement and prediction sites.

### 1.3.6 windPRO as the Practical Frontend

In practice, WAsP is rarely used as a standalone program for energy yield assessments. Instead, it is typically accessed through a comprehensive software platform such as windPRO (developed by EMD International A/S). windPRO provides the complete project management environment for wind farm development, including:

- Terrain data import and management (elevation maps, roughness maps from various sources)
- Measurement station definition and data management
- Automated calls to the WAsP calculation engine for wind resource calculations
- Cross-prediction computations (predicting the wind climate at one mast from another mast's data)
- Wind farm layout design and optimisation
- Energy production calculations with power curve integration
- Wake loss modelling
- Noise and shadow flicker calculations

All cross-predictions analysed in this thesis were generated using the windPRO/WAsP software combination. The wind data were imported into windPRO, measurement stations and turbine positions were defined, and windPRO invoked the WAsP engine to compute the predicted wind climates at each target location. This workflow is representative of standard industry practice and ensures that the results of this thesis are directly applicable to operational EYA processes.

## 1.4 Terrain Complexity and the RIX Framework

### 1.4.1 The Challenge of Quantifying Terrain Complexity

As described in the previous section, the accuracy of WAsP predictions depends critically on the complexity of the terrain in which the measurement and prediction sites are located. In gentle terrain with smooth topography and homogeneous roughness, WAsP's linearised flow model provides accurate predictions, and the horizontal extrapolation uncertainty is relatively small. As terrain complexity increases -- with steeper slopes, deeper valleys, more rugged topography, and more heterogeneous roughness conditions -- the model's predictions become less reliable, and the uncertainty associated with horizontal extrapolation increases.

This raises a fundamental question: how can terrain complexity be quantified in a way that is useful for predicting the expected accuracy of a wind flow model? The answer requires a metric that is objective, computable from available terrain data, and correlated with the magnitude of prediction errors observed in practice. The most widely used metric for this purpose in the wind energy industry is the Ruggedness Index (RIX), developed by Mortensen and Petersen [3].

### 1.4.2 RIX: The Ruggedness Index

The Ruggedness Index (RIX) is defined as the fraction of the terrain surface within a specified radius of a given point where the terrain slope exceeds a critical threshold [3]. More precisely, for a given point, a set of radial lines is drawn outward in all directions (or in specific direction sectors). Along each radial line, the terrain slope is computed at each point. The RIX value is the fraction of the total length of all radial lines where the slope exceeds the critical threshold.

The concept behind RIX is straightforward: it measures how much of the terrain surrounding a site exceeds the steepness threshold beyond which WAsP's linearised model is expected to break down. A higher RIX value indicates more rugged terrain with more areas of steep slopes, and therefore a greater likelihood of non-linear flow effects (such as flow separation) that WAsP cannot capture.

Different slope thresholds are used in practice, depending on the application:

- **Slope threshold of 0.3 (approximately 17 degrees):** This is the original threshold proposed by Mortensen and Petersen, corresponding to the approximate limit of validity of the WAsP flow model. RIX computed with this threshold directly measures the fraction of terrain where WAsP is expected to produce unreliable results. This threshold is commonly used in the WAsP software itself and in many research applications.
- **Slope threshold of 0.0501 (approximately 2.9 degrees):** This lower threshold captures gentler terrain undulations that, while not steep enough to cause flow separation, still represent terrain complexity that can affect prediction accuracy. This threshold is more sensitive to moderate terrain complexity and provides a more graduated measure of terrain ruggedness.
- **Slope threshold of 0.033 (approximately 1.9 degrees):** This threshold is used in the FGW Technical Guideline TR6 for the computation of the Transfer RIX (T-RIX), as discussed in Section 1.4.4.

The RIX value is typically computed per wind direction sector (using 12 sectors of 30 degrees each), reflecting the fact that terrain complexity can vary significantly with direction. A site on a ridge, for example, may have low RIX in directions along the ridge axis and high RIX in directions perpendicular to the ridge. The sector-wise computation of RIX allows the directional characteristics of terrain complexity to be captured, which is important for understanding which wind direction sectors contribute most to the overall prediction uncertainty.

The range of RIX values encountered in practice spans from 0% (perfectly flat terrain, such as open plains or offshore locations) to values exceeding 50% (highly complex mountainous terrain with many steep slopes). As a rough classification:

- RIX below approximately 5%: flat to gently rolling terrain
- RIX between 5% and 15%: moderately complex terrain
- RIX between 15% and 30%: complex terrain
- RIX above 30%: very complex terrain

It should be noted that these thresholds are approximate and depend on the slope threshold used for the RIX computation, the radius over which RIX is computed, and the resolution of the terrain elevation data.

### 1.4.3 Delta-RIX (dRIX): Measuring Terrain Mismatch

While RIX characterises the absolute terrain complexity at a single site, a closely related metric -- delta-RIX (dRIX) -- is used to quantify the terrain mismatch between two sites, specifically the measurement site and the prediction site.

Delta-RIX is defined as the difference in RIX values between the two sites:

dRIX = RIX_predictor - RIX_predicted

where RIX_predictor is the RIX value at the measurement (predictor) site and RIX_predicted is the RIX value at the target (predicted) site. This difference is computed per direction sector to capture directional variations in terrain mismatch.

The interpretation of dRIX is as follows:

- **dRIX = 0:** The two sites have the same terrain complexity (at least as measured by the RIX metric). WAsP's error in removing terrain effects at the predictor site is expected to be similar in magnitude and direction to its error in applying terrain effects at the predicted site, so the errors may partially cancel.
- **dRIX > 0:** The predictor site is in more complex terrain than the predicted site. WAsP may over-correct for terrain effects (removing too much at the predictor and adding too little at the predicted site), potentially leading to under-prediction of wind speeds at the target.
- **dRIX < 0:** The predicted site is in more complex terrain than the predictor site. The opposite bias may occur, with potential over-prediction of wind speeds at the target.

Delta-RIX has been proposed as a correction factor for WAsP predictions in complex terrain [3]. The idea is that the WAsP prediction error is approximately linearly related to dRIX: by measuring the dRIX between two sites, one can estimate the expected direction and approximate magnitude of the WAsP prediction bias. Several studies have investigated this relationship and found empirically that the WAsP prediction error (expressed as a percentage deviation of predicted from observed wind speed) is approximately proportional to dRIX, with a slope on the order of 1-3% per percent of dRIX, depending on the dataset and the definition of RIX.

However, the dRIX correction approach has significant limitations. The relationship between dRIX and prediction error is noisy and varies between sites, making it unreliable for precise corrections. Moreover, dRIX addresses only one aspect of the terrain mismatch (the difference in slope steepness) and does not capture other important factors such as roughness differences, distance effects, or measurement data quality.

### 1.4.4 T-RIX and the FGW TR6 Guideline

The FGW Technical Guideline TR6 [4] provides a standardised framework for wind resource assessment in Germany and is increasingly referenced in other European markets. The guideline defines the Transfer RIX (T-RIX) as a composite metric for evaluating the suitability of horizontal extrapolation between a measurement site and a target site.

The T-RIX is defined as:

T-RIX = 0.9 * (RIX_A + RIX_B) / 2 + 0.1 * |dz|

where RIX_A and RIX_B are the RIX values (computed with a slope threshold of 0.033) at the measurement and target sites respectively, (RIX_A + RIX_B) / 2 is the average RIX of the two sites, and |dz| is the absolute height difference between the two sites in metres.

The T-RIX combines two aspects of the extrapolation challenge:

1. **Terrain complexity:** The average RIX of the two sites, weighted at 0.9, captures the overall ruggedness of the terrain in which the extrapolation is performed.
2. **Height difference:** The absolute height difference |dz|, weighted at 0.1, captures the vertical aspect of the transfer -- sites with a large elevation difference are more challenging to model than sites at similar elevations.

The FGW TR6 guideline uses the T-RIX to define two critical distances for horizontal extrapolation:

- **Distance A (lower bound):** distance_A = (-0.087 * T-RIX + 8.5) * 1000 metres. Within this distance, horizontal extrapolation using a linearised flow model (WAsP) is considered acceptable, provided that basic quality criteria for the terrain data and measurement campaign are met.
- **Distance B (upper bound):** distance_B = (-0.14 * T-RIX + 15) * 1000 metres. Beyond this distance, horizontal extrapolation is generally not recommended without additional justification (e.g., comparison with CFD results, additional measurement points, or detailed analysis of the prediction uncertainty).

Between distances A and B, horizontal extrapolation is considered acceptable but requires additional caution, including a more detailed assessment of the prediction uncertainty and potentially the use of correction factors or supplementary analysis.

The terrain classification based on T-RIX can be approximately summarised as follows (noting that these boundaries are not precisely defined in the guideline and are used here for illustrative purposes):

- **Flat terrain (T-RIX < approximately 40%):** Gentle hills, agricultural land, and open landscapes. WAsP predictions are generally reliable, and horizontal extrapolation can be performed over relatively long distances (up to 8-10 km for the most favourable conditions).
- **Semi-complex terrain (T-RIX approximately 40-50%):** Rolling hills, forest edges, and moderate topographic relief. WAsP predictions are adequate but less accurate than in flat terrain, and the acceptable extrapolation distance is reduced (typically 4-6 km).
- **Complex terrain (T-RIX > approximately 50%):** Mountain ridges, steep valleys, and highly variable topography. WAsP predictions may be unreliable, and horizontal extrapolation distances should be minimised (typically less than 3-4 km). Additional analysis with non-linear flow models (CFD) is often recommended.

### 1.4.5 Limitations of the RIX Framework

While the RIX framework provides a useful and widely adopted metric for terrain classification and a rough guide to the expected accuracy of linear flow models, it has significant limitations as a tool for predicting site-specific horizontal extrapolation uncertainty:

1. **Single-parameter characterisation:** RIX reduces the complex, multi-dimensional problem of terrain characterisation to a single number (or one number per sector). It captures terrain steepness but not other important terrain features such as roughness heterogeneity, distance from the coast, valley geometry, or the orientation of terrain features relative to the prevailing wind direction.

2. **No measurement quality information:** RIX is a purely terrain-based metric and contains no information about the quality or characteristics of the measurement data used in the extrapolation. Two pairs with identical RIX values may have very different prediction errors if one pair has a high-quality measurement campaign with a tall mast at the hub height and the other has a short mast with questionable data quality.

3. **Limited discrimination:** In practice, many wind farm sites fall into the "semi-complex" category where the RIX values are moderate. Within this category, RIX provides limited discrimination between sites that will be predicted accurately and those that will not. Other factors -- such as the specific terrain features between the sites, the roughness conditions, the wind direction distribution, and the atmospheric stability regime -- may be more important than the RIX value alone in determining prediction accuracy.

4. **No probabilistic output:** The RIX framework and the TR6 guideline provide distance thresholds and terrain classifications, but they do not provide a quantitative, probabilistic estimate of the prediction uncertainty for a specific site pair. The energy yield assessor must still assign an uncertainty value based on expert judgement, using the RIX classification as one input among many.

These limitations motivate the present work. The central hypothesis of this thesis is that by combining multiple terrain, site, and data quality features -- including but not limited to RIX and dRIX -- in a data-driven statistical model, it is possible to produce more accurate, site-specific predictions of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty than can be achieved using the RIX framework alone.


## 1.5 Current Approaches to Horizontal Uncertainty Quantification

### 1.5.1 Industry Practice: Qualitative Assessment and Fixed Percentage Adders

The dominant approach to horizontal extrapolation uncertainty quantification in current industry practice is based on expert judgement and fixed percentage adders. In a typical EYA, the energy yield assessor reviews the terrain characteristics of the project site, the measurement campaign configuration, and the distance between the measurement and turbine locations, and then assigns a horizontal extrapolation uncertainty value based on a combination of experience, internal guidelines, and published benchmarks.

Common approaches include:

- **Terrain class-based adders:** The assessor classifies the site terrain as flat, semi-complex, or complex (often using RIX or T-RIX as a guide) and assigns a corresponding uncertainty value. Typical ranges might be:
  - Flat terrain: 2-3% of energy yield
  - Semi-complex terrain: 4-6% of energy yield
  - Complex terrain: 6-10% of energy yield

- **Distance-based adjustments:** The base uncertainty is increased for sites where the distance between the measurement mast and the turbine positions is large, reflecting the expectation that prediction accuracy decreases with distance.

- **CFD comparison adjustments:** If a CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) model has been run as a cross-check on the WAsP results, the assessor may adjust the uncertainty based on the agreement or disagreement between the two models.

- **Internal validation databases:** Some consultancies maintain internal databases of cross-prediction results from past projects, which they use to calibrate their uncertainty estimates. However, these databases are typically proprietary and not standardised across the industry.

The strengths of this qualitative approach are its simplicity, flexibility, and the fact that it allows the assessor to incorporate project-specific factors that may not be captured by any quantitative model. An experienced assessor can account for unusual terrain features, data quality issues, or other site-specific considerations that affect prediction uncertainty.

However, the weaknesses of this approach are significant:

- **Subjectivity:** Different assessors -- or different consultancies -- may assign different uncertainty values to the same site, leading to inconsistency in the market. A recent industry survey found that horizontal extrapolation uncertainty estimates for the same project could vary by a factor of two or more between different EYA providers.

- **Lack of site specificity:** Fixed percentage adders based on broad terrain classifications cannot capture the nuances of individual sites. Two sites that are both classified as "complex terrain" may have very different actual prediction errors, depending on the specific terrain geometry, roughness conditions, measurement configuration, and distance. Assigning the same uncertainty to both sites is inherently imprecise.

- **Conservative bias:** Because the consequences of under-estimating uncertainty are more severe than over-estimating it (an overly optimistic EYA can lead to project failure, while an overly conservative EYA merely reduces the project's apparent financial attractiveness), there is a natural tendency toward conservatism. This conservatism may be appropriate for risk management but is economically inefficient: projects with genuinely low uncertainty are penalised by receiving the same uncertainty adder as projects with genuinely high uncertainty.

- **No formal uncertainty on the uncertainty:** The assigned uncertainty value is a point estimate with no formal quantification of its own uncertainty. The assessor does not typically provide a probability distribution over the uncertainty estimate, making it difficult for the project's financial stakeholders to evaluate how reliable the uncertainty estimate itself is.

### 1.5.2 Deterministic Correction Approaches

A second category of approaches focuses on correcting the mean prediction of WAsP, rather than quantifying the uncertainty around it. These approaches recognise that WAsP has systematic biases in complex terrain and seek to remove or reduce these biases through deterministic corrections.

**dRIX-based corrections.** As described in Section 1.4.3, the relationship between delta-RIX (dRIX) and WAsP prediction error has been used to develop empirical correction functions. The typical approach is to fit a linear regression between dRIX and observed prediction error across a dataset of cross-prediction pairs, and then to apply this regression to correct WAsP predictions at new sites based on their computed dRIX values [3]. While this approach can reduce the mean bias in WAsP predictions, it does not address the scatter around the mean -- that is, it does not provide an uncertainty estimate. Moreover, the dRIX-error relationship varies between datasets and regions, limiting its generalisability.

**CFD cross-checks.** Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) models solve the full Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations for turbulent flow, capturing non-linear effects such as flow separation and recirculation that WAsP cannot model. In complex terrain, CFD models may provide more accurate predictions than WAsP, and the difference between WAsP and CFD predictions can be used as an indicator of terrain complexity and potential WAsP bias. However, CFD models are significantly more computationally expensive than WAsP, require more detailed input data and expert setup, and have their own sources of uncertainty (turbulence model selection, boundary conditions, mesh resolution, and domain size). CFD is therefore typically used as a supplementary analysis tool rather than as the primary basis for the EYA, and it does not directly provide a quantitative uncertainty estimate.

**Mesoscale-microscale coupling.** Recent developments in wind resource assessment have introduced mesoscale-microscale coupling approaches, where a mesoscale atmospheric model (such as WRF) is used to provide boundary conditions or downscaling for the microscale flow model (WAsP or CFD). This coupling can help to account for mesoscale climate variations between the measurement and prediction sites, which is one of the sources of error that the Similarity Principle cannot address. However, these approaches are still in development, are computationally demanding, and do not yet provide a standardised framework for uncertainty quantification.

### 1.5.3 The Research Gap

The review of current approaches reveals a significant gap in the state of the art for horizontal extrapolation uncertainty quantification. Specifically:

- **No existing method provides a data-driven, site-specific, probabilistic estimate of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty.** Current industry practice relies on expert judgement and fixed adders, which are inherently imprecise and subjective. Deterministic correction approaches (dRIX corrections, CFD cross-checks) address the mean bias but not the uncertainty.

- **The available terrain complexity metrics (RIX, T-RIX) capture only one dimension of the problem.** Terrain steepness is an important factor, but horizontal extrapolation uncertainty also depends on roughness conditions, distance, wind direction distribution, measurement data quality, and other factors that are not captured by a single terrain metric.

- **There is no systematic framework for combining multiple terrain and site features into a comprehensive uncertainty prediction.** The relationships between individual features and prediction uncertainty have been studied in isolation, but no existing model integrates multiple features into a single, calibrated uncertainty estimate.

What is needed is a model that:
1. Takes as input a set of measurable terrain, site, and data quality characteristics -- features that can be computed from available data before the prediction is made.
2. Produces as output a site-specific, probabilistic estimate of the expected horizontal extrapolation uncertainty -- not a fixed adder, but a prediction tailored to the specific characteristics of the prediction pair.
3. Provides uncertainty on the uncertainty estimate itself -- quantifying how confident the model is in its prediction.
4. Is validated against real cross-prediction data, with appropriate safeguards against data leakage and overfitting.

### 1.5.4 Why Bayesian Methods Are Appropriate

The development of such a model poses several statistical challenges that make Bayesian methods particularly suitable:

**Small datasets.** The dataset available for this thesis consists of approximately 14 independent physical pairs (measurement site pairs where both sites have concurrent wind measurements, allowing the accuracy of cross-predictions to be evaluated), yielding 28 directional observations. The effective sample size is small. Classical frequentist methods (ordinary least squares regression, maximum likelihood estimation) require larger sample sizes to produce reliable estimates, particularly when the number of candidate features is large relative to the sample size. Bayesian methods, by contrast, can produce meaningful results with small samples by incorporating prior information about the expected behaviour of the model parameters. These priors encode domain knowledge -- for example, the physical expectation that more complex terrain should increase, not decrease, prediction uncertainty -- and regularise the estimation, preventing the model from overfitting to the limited data.

**Structured data with directional pairing.** The data in this thesis have a natural structure: each physical pair of measurement sites produces two directional observations (A predicts B, and B predicts A), sharing the same terrain context, distance, and height difference but with direction-dependent energy-weighted features. The model operates at the pair level rather than the sector level, training directly on the omnidirectional energy-weighted error -- the quantity that matters for energy yield assessment. This pair-level formulation avoids the need for hierarchical nesting of sectors within pairs, resulting in a simpler model that targets the correct quantity. The Bayesian framework remains essential for handling the small effective sample size (~14 independent physical pairs) through informative priors that encode domain knowledge and provide regularisation [5].

**Physical constraints as priors.** A key advantage of Bayesian methods is the ability to incorporate domain knowledge directly into the model through the choice of prior distributions. In this application, there are strong physical reasons to expect that certain features should increase uncertainty (e.g., higher terrain complexity, greater distance between sites, larger roughness heterogeneity) and no physical reason to expect that they should decrease it. These constraints can be implemented by placing HalfNormal priors (or other non-negative priors) on the relevant model coefficients, ensuring that the model's predictions are physically sensible even when the data are insufficient to determine the sign of a coefficient empirically. This is much more difficult to achieve in a frequentist framework, where parameter constraints must be imposed through constrained optimisation, which is less flexible and harder to interpret.

**Full posterior distributions.** Bayesian inference produces full posterior distributions over all model parameters, not just point estimates. This means that the model naturally provides uncertainty on its own predictions -- including uncertainty on the uncertainty estimate. For a model that predicts uncertainty, this is a critical feature: it allows the user to know not only the best estimate of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty for a given site pair, but also how confident the model is in that estimate. Full posterior distributions also enable rigorous model comparison, sensitivity analysis, and prediction interval estimation through posterior predictive checks.

**Regularisation for many candidate features.** The feature exploration process in this thesis involves testing 20 or more candidate terrain and site features as potential predictors of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty. With only approximately 14 independent physical pairs, there is a severe risk of overfitting -- finding spurious relationships between features and uncertainty that do not generalise to new sites. Bayesian priors provide a natural form of regularisation: by shrinking parameter estimates toward zero (or toward physically motivated values), priors prevent the model from fitting noise in the training data. This is analogous to ridge regression or LASSO regularisation in frequentist statistics, but with the added advantage that the degree of regularisation is determined by the priors rather than by a separate tuning parameter, and the full posterior distribution of each parameter provides a natural measure of whether that feature is genuinely informative.


## 1.6 Research Objectives and Thesis Structure

### 1.6.1 Primary Research Objective

The primary objective of this thesis is to develop a Bayesian model that predicts the overall (omnidirectional) wind speed transfer uncertainty for horizontal extrapolation as a function of measurable terrain, site, and data quality characteristics. The model takes as input a set of features that can be computed from available data -- terrain elevation maps, roughness maps, measurement station metadata, and the output of WAsP calculations -- and produces as output a probabilistic estimate of the expected wind speed prediction uncertainty (sigma) for each measurement-to-turbine transfer, along with a bias estimate driven by the height difference between the two sites.

The model operates at the pair level, training directly on the energy-weighted omnidirectional wind speed error -- the quantity that determines the energy yield impact. Sector-level terrain and wind features (such as turning, speedup, and roughness corrections) are aggregated to the pair level using energy-based weighting, ensuring that sectors contributing most to energy production receive proportionally greater influence on the uncertainty estimate.

The model is designed to be used in the pre-construction phase of a wind energy project, when the measurement campaign has been completed and WAsP predictions have been generated but before the project is financed and built. At this stage, the model enables the energy yield assessor to replace the conventional fixed percentage adder with a site-specific, data-driven uncertainty estimate that accounts for the specific terrain, distance, roughness, and data quality characteristics of the project. This more accurate uncertainty estimate leads to more accurate P-values, which in turn enable better-informed investment decisions and fairer financing terms for projects with genuinely low uncertainty.

### 1.6.2 Secondary Objectives

In support of the primary objective, this thesis pursues several secondary objectives:

**1. Build an automated data pipeline from raw measurements to model-ready features.** The analysis in this thesis requires processing raw 10-minute wind measurement data from multiple measurement campaigns, computing cross-predictions using windPRO/WAsP, extracting terrain and site features from various data sources, and assembling these into a structured dataset suitable for statistical modelling. This data pipeline must be automated, reproducible, and extensible, so that new measurement campaigns can be incorporated into the analysis as they become available. The development of this pipeline is a significant practical contribution of the thesis, as it establishes the infrastructure for ongoing model development and validation.

**2. Identify the dominant physical drivers of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty.** Through systematic feature exploration and ablation testing, this thesis seeks to determine which terrain, site, and data quality characteristics are the most important predictors of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty. Understanding these drivers provides insight into the physical mechanisms that cause prediction errors and can guide the wind energy industry in identifying sites and configurations where uncertainty is expected to be high or low. The feature exploration process involves examining a wide range of candidate features, including terrain complexity metrics (RIX, dRIX, T-RIX), distance between sites, roughness characteristics, WAsP speedup and deflection values, wind speed and direction distributions, measurement campaign duration and data recovery, and various derived and interaction features.

**3. Validate the model through leave-one-out cross-validation with two holdout strategies.** Model validation is critical for establishing the credibility and reliability of the uncertainty predictions. With a small dataset of approximately 14 independent physical pairs, conventional train-test splitting is impractical. Instead, this thesis employs two leave-one-out cross-validation strategies: (a) pair-level holdout, where both directional observations of a physical pair are held out together, and (b) site-level holdout, where all pairs associated with a given location are held out simultaneously. Site-level holdout is the stricter test, as it evaluates the model's performance on entirely unseen locations -- the realistic deployment scenario. Both strategies ensure that the held-out data were not used during model training, preventing data leakage. The cross-validation results are evaluated using calibration metrics (do the predicted uncertainty intervals have the correct coverage?) and accuracy metrics (how well does the model predict the observed prediction errors?).

**4. Create a deployment-ready uncertainty calculator for industry use.** The ultimate goal of this thesis is to produce a tool that can be used by wind energy practitioners in their day-to-day EYA work. The uncertainty calculator takes as input the terrain and site features of a new prediction pair and outputs the predicted horizontal extrapolation uncertainty, along with confidence intervals on that estimate. The calculator is implemented as a software tool that can be integrated into the existing EYA workflow, requiring minimal additional effort from the user beyond what is already done in a standard windPRO/WAsP analysis.

### 1.6.3 Scope Boundaries

To maintain focus and clarity, the scope of this thesis is bounded as follows:

- **Onshore wind energy only.** The terrain effects, flow modelling approaches, and uncertainty characteristics addressed in this thesis are specific to onshore wind energy. Offshore wind energy involves different physical processes (e.g., air-sea interaction, coastal transitions, limited fetch effects) and different flow modelling tools, and is not addressed here.
- **WAsP-based predictions.** The model is developed and validated specifically for horizontal extrapolation performed using the WAsP linearised flow model (accessed through windPRO). The results may not directly apply to predictions made using other flow models (e.g., CFD, mesoscale models, or other microscale models), although the general approach and many of the features explored in this thesis may be transferable.
- **Wind speed uncertainty as the primary target.** The model predicts the uncertainty in the predicted wind speed (or, equivalently, the wind speed deviation between predicted and observed values). While wind speed uncertainty is a major component of energy yield uncertainty, the relationship between wind speed uncertainty and energy yield uncertainty also involves the power curve, the wind speed distribution, and other factors that are not directly modelled here. The conversion from wind speed uncertainty to energy yield uncertainty is straightforward in principle (through the power curve sensitivity) but depends on the specific turbine and wind conditions.
- **Uncertainty magnitude with limited bias modelling.** The model's primary output is the predicted uncertainty magnitude (sigma). Additionally, the model includes a bias term driven by the signed height difference (dz) between the measurement and target sites, capturing the systematic tendency of WAsP to over- or under-predict wind speed when extrapolating between sites at different elevations. This bias term is modest in scope -- it addresses only the elevation-driven component of WAsP's systematic errors, not the full range of terrain-dependent biases.
- **Features available at deployment.** An important practical constraint is that the model must use only features that are available at the time of deployment -- that is, features that can be computed from the predictor-site (measurement mast) data, the terrain data, and the WAsP output, without requiring observed data at the target (turbine) site. This constraint rules out features that require observed Weibull parameters or wind data at the target site, since such data are not available in the standard EYA workflow where the goal is to predict the wind climate at an unmeasured location.

### 1.6.4 Thesis Structure

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows:

**Chapter 2: Methodology.** This chapter describes the complete methodology of the research, including the data pipeline (from raw measurements to model-ready features), the feature extraction process, the evolution of the Bayesian model through successive iterations, the specification of the final model (priors, likelihood, energy-weighted feature aggregation, and bias model), the validation strategy (leave-one-out cross-validation at both pair and site level), and the software tools and computational methods used.

**Chapter 3: Results.** This chapter presents the results of the analysis, including the model's predictive performance (calibration curves, coverage statistics, and accuracy metrics), the estimated feature importance and posterior distributions of model parameters, comparison with conventional industry approaches (fixed adders and RIX-based estimates), and the results of feature ablation tests that identify the most important drivers of horizontal extrapolation uncertainty.

**Chapter 4: Discussion.** This chapter interprets the results in the context of the existing literature and industry practice, discusses the physical interpretation of the identified uncertainty drivers, examines the limitations of the model and the dataset, considers the sensitivity of the results to modelling choices, and compares the Bayesian approach with alternative statistical methods.

**Chapter 5: Conclusion and Outlook.** This chapter summarises the key findings of the thesis, states the main conclusions, provides recommendations for industry practitioners, and identifies directions for future research, including potential extensions of the model, additional data sources that could improve its performance, and the applicability of the approach to other components of EYA uncertainty.
